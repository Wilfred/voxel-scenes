# Voxel Scenes art direction

Voxel Scenes generates a fresh landscape for a desktop wallpaper each day.
Unrailed, Tiny Glade, Woodo, and DeCube are visual references. Aim for
chunky voxel shapes, tactile surfaces, and terrain with a silhouette that
reads at thumbnail size. Leave some quieter space, such as an open meadow or
calm water, for desktop icons.

Use the [Tiny Voxel Nature Pack](https://www.gamedevmarket.net/asset/tiny-voxel-nature-pack)
as a reference for how coarse the blocks in a woodland prop can be.
For wildlife, aim for a few shapes that identify the animal from the wallpaper
camera, as in [Voxel Tiny Animals](https://vox-fox.itch.io/voxel-tiny-animals).

For a sense of miniature scale, look at Four-Plate Fig Scale (1:120) and
smaller in [ACT Museum's 12 Scales of LEGO](https://actmuseum.org/2025/01/28/12-scales-of-lego/).
A walker can read from a head and coat, and a distant cottage from its roof,
chimney, and doorway. Choose the few features still visible across the valley.

## Vistas and dioramas

A vista looks out across a landscape that continues beyond the frame.
A river bend can lead the eye through a wooded valley, or a series of
overlapping ridges can draw it toward a distant peak.

A diorama shows a whole place from a pulled-back viewpoint, with room
around its outline. An island might curl around a sheltered cove. On a
floating island, the stepped rock underneath matters as much to the
silhouette as the woods on top.

Either framing can suit any terrain. A chain of floating islands fading
into cloud makes a vista, while a forested watershed from spring to pond
could fit in one diorama. Vary the shape of the land from day to day.

## Camera and composition

Frame a vista through a narrow perspective lens, with a 36° vertical field of view, looking
down about 13° in the current sky study. Keep the focal building crisp and let haze close in on the
far terrain until it dissolves into the sky. Show some sky above the land.
Frame a rectangular model in parallel projection from higher up, so it reads
as a miniature on its plain background, with room around its whole outline.

Show the roof and two faces of a cottage from an elevated angle. Set the
focal point near a third of the frame, facing into the larger part, with a
path, shore, or stream leading the eye toward it. Leave breathing room above
a summit.

## Colour and light

Plan the image in values before hues. At thumbnail size, the land, the
woods, the water and the sky should separate into distinct masses.

- Use one grass hue across the scenes. Step its value with height, cooler and
  darker in hollows and warmer on crests, so relief reads before shadows do.
- Keep conifers darker and bluer than broadleaves, so stands read apart, and
  keep both distinct from the grass. Use restrained greens for ordinary
  woodland and reserve yellow foliage for a seasonal scene. Palm fronds are
  lighter and yellower than temperate leaves, and glossy tropical broadleaves
  sit between the palms and the pines. Plant tropical trees by their role:
  sea almonds behind a beach, breadfruit on higher ground and bananas in a
  cottage garden.
- Use one wood colour for living trunks and built timber. Let the warm roof
  tile be the main accent in a green landscape.
- In sunny weather, let direct sun outweigh sky light about two to one so cast
  shadows read crisply. Overcast light can be soft. Align cast shadows with
  the depicted sun.
- Give skies and water natural depth. A sky grades from the haze colour at
  the horizon to a deeper zenith. Shallow water shows the bed through actual
  transparency, and calm water reflects the nearby banks. Keep ripples broad
  and calm at wallpaper scale.
- Keep silhouettes clear through changes in weather.

## Signs of life

Keep the landscape sparsely inhabited. A single cottage with smoke rising
from its chimney, two walkers on a mountain saddle, or a few deer peeking
out between the trees can give the whole scene a sense of scale. Let the
terrain catch the eye first and leave these details for a second glance.

Give each animal a reason to be there. Gulls float in the shallows along a
quiet shore, all facing into the wind that carries the chimney smoke, and fly
over land, where they show white against the trees. Ducks swim out on open
water, clear of the bank, and deer graze where woodland opens out. Sheep and
cattle graze in fields beside a track. Moor a
rowing boat by its bow to a pier, since a lone boat elsewhere looks adrift, and
sail a dinghy out to sea, clear of the landing. Spread these details across the scene
rather than gathering them by the cottage. Make suitable sites more likely
while leaving room for variation. Keep flocks loose and give pairs enough space
for their silhouettes to separate.

Let fences explain land use. At the mill, the paddock gate faces the cart
track. Fence a field only where its animals stand in it, and gather fields near
the buildings and each other, with horses kept close to the house or mill. On
steep paths a short guardrail marks the drop. A kitchen-garden fence is another
direction to explore when a cottage has a cultivated patch to enclose.

## Weather and seasons

Give the daily views a range of weather and light. Mist can soften the
farthest ridge while leaving the near slopes crisp. Sun after rain can
pick out a stream winding through dark pines.

The current sky study uses distant banks with flat bases, stepped outlines
and a cooler underside, inspired by Minecraft clouds. Leave gaps above the
horizon and between banks. Use the same sun direction for lit faces, cast
shadows and water highlights. Sunrise can pick out a cool ridge against a
peach horizon; late light should catch the opposite faces. Make clearing rain
visible against the landscape while giving the cottage room to read.

At particular times of year, let the season influence the whole landscape.
The orchard already blossoms in spring and the mill wears a December wreath.
Further cues are still to come: around Christmas, fresh snow might trace the
terraces of a hillside, and around Halloween, low light might throw long tree
shadows across a clearing. A few warm lights on a fir or a pumpkin lantern by
a cottage door can reward a closer look. Keep them small enough to belong in
the scene at its natural scale.

## Model proportions

Use two metres per world unit. A half-metre masonry voxel is 0.25 world units.
Cottage walls occupy 14 × 10 masonry voxels (7 × 5 metres), and a doorway is
two voxels wide and four high. Trees use the same half-metre voxel. These dimensions describe the depicted landscape. The camera and screen
size determine its apparent miniature scale.

Treat the scale as a guideline. These are decorative images, not accurate
simulations, so draw small animals larger than life until the shapes that
identify them read from the wallpaper camera: a sheep's dark face, a duck's
head and bill. Keep their order of size, so a sheep stays smaller than a
deer. As a starting point, sheep are two fifths larger than life, and
swimming ducks and gulls about 0.9 metres from tail to bill. Leave out
animals and props that would still be dots at that scale, such as hens,
rabbits, crates and hives.
The deer, cows, horses, boats, cottage and trees stay at their true scale.

Use the Stone bothy and Orchard house as cottage references. Keep the roof
silhouette clear and align projections such as porches and bays to the brick
grid. Give the cottage a legible entrance, softly contrasting windows, and its
familiar chimney smoke.

Give woodland trees a trunk one voxel across, with one to one and a half
metres of it visible below simple, full crowns, except where a long bare trunk
identifies the tree, as in a Scots or stone pine. Centre every tier on the
trunk with an odd width, so the crown is symmetric, and round the corners of
wide tiers in whole voxels. Most adult crowns are three to four and a half
metres across and six to seven metres tall. Taper conifers to a single-voxel
leader so they read apart from broadleaves in silhouette. Model saplings and
adults separately, at about half the adult height. Grow trees in stands with
irregular gaps and room for their crowns, separated by open meadows.

Give larger landscapes a few working buildings on gentle ground beside a route
that explains their presence.

Keep a cottage door visible from the wallpaper camera and connect it to its
approach. Give the entrance depth with a recessed door and low stone sill.
Let open-sided working buildings reveal the space where their work happens.

Around waterfalls, establish the grassy valley, bedrock and soil cover before
carving the channel. Exposed stone should continue naturally from an outcrop
into the bank or streambed. Draw falling water as uneven, chunky whitewater
rather than regular bars.

## When to subdivide a voxel

Terrain, paths and stair rises follow their terrain lattice. Buildings, trees
and built structures share one 0.25-world-unit construction voxel (half a metre
in the landscape), so a crown and a wall beside it show blocks of the same
size. Decks, piles, rails and walls use multiples of that unit across scenes.
Adapt a structure’s span and width to its job while keeping the same thickness
for comparable members.

The half-course, a quarter of a metre, is the one shared subdivision. Use it for
a small functional detail such as a signboard, sill or window reveal, in trees
and buildings alike. Animals and hand-sized props such as creels can
use their own finer grid so their legs and heads read at the established body
size. Keep a single detail scale within each model.
Water, foam and smoke can depart from the solid grid for surface and air effects.

For cottage windows, use the deep reveals and paired lights in the Chedworth
cottage photographs as a guide. At wallpaper scale one inset pane, or a pair
with a single mullion, can describe the opening. Let the wall material form
the sill and jambs. [Village cottage](https://www.geograph.org.uk/photo/223590)
and [Church Row](https://www.geograph.org.uk/photo/6379744).
